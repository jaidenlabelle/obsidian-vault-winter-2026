## Entrepreneurial Beginnings

Gabe Newell, while working at Microsoft, was having a challenge trying to convince people that not every app needed its own approach to graphics acceleration tech, and that games would not lose performance by moving Windows. The people most resistant to Newell's ideas were those in the PC games industry, with developers adamant they knew best how to get their games playing with graphics tech.

At the time Doom was selling so well, it seemed to be outperforming Microsoft's sales of Windows. Gabe Newell saw an opportunity and contacted the developers of Doom and offered to port their game to Windows for free, to prove that you can have very high performance running videogames on Windows.

He was successful in overcoming this challenge, and after he contacting Dooms developer he started thinking about making a video game company of his own. Eventually founding Valve.

1. Half-Life, a first-person shooter with horror elements, was released in November 1998. It was a disruptive game in the industry, in 2014 IGN stated that the history of the FPS genre "breaks down pretty cleanly into pre-Half-Life and post-Half-Life eras" [2].

3. Counter-Strike, a first-person multiplayer shooter, was released in 1999 as well. It was a incremental innovation, based on a GoldSrc mod.
4. Half-Life 2 and Source, a sequel to the original Half-Life and the in-house engine developed to create it, was released in 2004. It was a disruptive innovation, it had advanced physics systems not seen before in games of the time. By 2011, it had sold 12 million copies.
5. Steam, a digital storefront and delivery platform, was released in 2002. It initially only offered Valve games, and was required to install Half-Life 2. But soon it became a publisher of third-party games. It was a disruptive innovation, and generates the vast majority of Valves revenue today.
